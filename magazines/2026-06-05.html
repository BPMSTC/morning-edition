Teaching AI agents to ask better questions by playing “Battleship”

# MIT Researchers Find Battleship Teaches AI Agents Information-Theoretic Discipline

MIT researchers trained AI agents to play Battleship—the board game where you sink ships by guessing coordinates—and discovered that smaller models with proper training beat larger ones at the task while burning a fraction of the compute. The game forces agents to minimize uncertainty efficiently: each query either eliminates grid cells or narrows ship positions, creating hard constraints on what constitutes a "good" question. The researchers used this as a training regime and found that a small model trained this way outperformed GPT-3.5-scale models on the task at roughly 1% of the computational cost. Battleship works here because it's a closed, measurable problem where you can objectively score question quality—no hand-waving required.

This matters because current LLMs are notoriously bad at strategic information-gathering; they'll ask redundant or tangential questions when a smarter query exists. Training on games with hard information-theoretic feedback loops could genuinely improve agent behavior in domains where question-asking matters—customer support, scientific hypothesis design, debugging. The practical upside is real: if you can get comparable or better performance from smaller models through the right training approach, that's not a rounding error in ops costs. The caveat: Battleship's perfect information and discrete state space are luxuries most real problems don't have. Still, this is the kind of incremental, reproducible research that actually moves capabilities forward rather than just scaling existing approaches into the ground.
